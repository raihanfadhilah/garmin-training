Block 01 · 11 weeks

Madeira — the descent problem

The single decision that decides this race

Descend the first steep section at an effort where you could hold a conversation and stop within two strides. If you are passing people there, you are going too fast. Across 23,207 finishers in 56 races, later finishers over-spent time in the first segment in 91% of them — after the opening, everyone paces the remainder alike. The damage is done at the start.

Why the peak sits 15 days out

A meta-analysis of 14 studies found the largest taper effect at 8–14 days, with a 41–60% volume cut beating both smaller and larger reductions, and progressive beating step. Pre-taper overload beat conventional taper. Week 15 is that overload. Expected gain: 2–3%, or 7–11 minutes on a six-hour race.

Hard stop

Nothing eccentric after 21 October. Last heavy lower-body work, last plyometrics, last downhill. Force loss takes 3 days to recover and soreness 4; anything closer arrives at the start line as fatigue rather than adaptation.

Vertical

Uphill intervals
from wk 9		 3–5 × (5 min hard @ 90–95% HRmax + 3 min easy erg). Target distribution is 80–85% easy / 15–20% hard, with the hard work on incline. The erg stations are upper-body and aerobic, so they add nothing to eccentric load — and they double as Hyrox compromised running.
The honest limit of all this

The repeated-bout effect protects soreness and CK, not strength. Ten downhill sessions across four weeks moved soreness from 23.6 mm to 6.7 mm — but knee-extensor strength loss went −13.9% → −9.3%, not significant throughout. You will reach the second climb with weakened quads regardless. That is why race-day descent pacing is co-equal with training, not a footnote.

In-race diagnostic

Compare your climb rate per heartbeat on the second climb against the first. More than 5% worse means you overcooked the descent — concede ten minutes immediately and save the day. In mountain ultras heart rate falls ~34 bpm across the race while RPE rises from 4.3 to 6.9; they decouple in opposite directions, so HR is a ceiling in the first third only.

Sled technique — the one to drill

Forearms hooked on the posts, 30–45° lean, short choppy steps of 20–30 cm at 3–4 per second, and never let it stop. At 56 kg you need 1.03× bodyweight of horizontal force to break a stationary sled versus 0.81× to keep one rolling. Grippy solid-rubber shoes are worth 30–60 s. Venue friction varies enough to swing any sled split by ±60–90 s.

Two beliefs the data killed

Even pacing is not a lever. Adding pacing-evenness to a fitness model changes R² by 0.0000. Optimal is a controlled 12–15% positive split; zero teams at any level produce an even one.
"Don't go out hard" is not visible either. Matched for fitness, teams with a fast first kilometre finish faster, and their run 8 is no slower.

Frequency beats volume

4 × 30–40 min beats 2 × 75 min, because fatigue compromises motor-pattern learning. Never let a gap exceed five days — students returning after breaks "often appear to forget everything they once knew." This is the single design decision the swim depends on.

The failure mode is breathing, not fitness

Hold breath → CO₂ builds → desperate gasp → head lifts → hips sink → work harder → more breathless. Continuous gentle exhale whenever the face is submerged is taught first and is non-negotiable. Of 120 sudden deaths in US triathlon, 90 occurred in the swim — the mechanism is panic in cold open water, not cardiac capacity.

Porridge, properly

Method · 4 min · microwave

Everything in the bowl at the start: 80 g oats, 300 ml volle melk, a pinch of salt, half a banana mashed in.

2 min → stir hard for 20 seconds → 1–2 min more.

Stir the peanut butter all the way through, not a dollop on top. Stand 1 minute. Slice the other half of the banana on top.

~700 kcal, ~95 g carbohydrate. Use a big bowl — it climbs.

The three things that fix it

Salt at the start. It does not make it salty — it makes the oats taste toasted and the banana taste sweeter. Most people skip it and wonder why porridge is bland.

Banana mashed in from the beginning, not halfway. It dissolves and sweetens the whole bowl instead of leaving sweet pockets.

Twenty seconds of hard stirring mid-cook. This replaces the five minutes of stovetop stirring that makes porridge creamy rather than lumpy. Stir like you mean it.

Pair within families. Berries and peanut butter fight — berries are tart and acidic, peanut butter is rich and savoury, and they pull in opposite directions.

Concentration is the rule people break

Keep drink mix at 6–8% — ~35 g of sugar per 500 ml, ~60 g per 750 ml. Above 500 mOsm/kg, 42.1% of a 10% drink was still in the stomach versus 0.3% of a 2% drink. A 500 ml flask carrying 80 g is 16% and a documented route to nausea.

Product

Upfront Gel PRO — 40 g carbs, 1:0.8 glucose:fructose, €2.33 each. That is ~€5.83 per 100 g of carbs against ~€11 for SIS Beta Fuel and ~€19 for Maurten, at identical spec.

The packaging is genuinely bad and unusable on the bike. Decant into a soft flask for running; use sugar-water bottles on the bike. Label the flask with contents and total kcal — every food item must carry its calorie count at kit check.

Train on DIY: maltodextrin + fructose at 1:0.8 is ~€1 per 100 g of carbs. Race on gels. Saves ~€66 across the Madeira block.

Sodium — largely unsupported

At Western States, hyponatraemic runners took 297 and 554 mg/h versus 515 ± 315 for non-hyponatraemic — p = 0.62. No difference between crampers and non-crampers either. Consensus guidance is drink to thirst (Grade 1C); sodium supplementation is not recommended for preventing hyponatraemia. A pinch of salt in the bottle is enough. Never combine salt capsules with fixed-schedule drinking beyond thirst — that combination is the documented route to the problem.

What the evidence actually says

The findings that drove decisions in this plan — including the ones that overturned an earlier version of it.

ClaimVerdictEvidence
A single 5-min weighted downhill walk confers protectionSupported−28% gradient, 5 km/h, 10% BW. One week later: strength loss −19.2% → −9.9%, CK 635 → 339 U/L, soreness 81.4 → 27.8 mm. Single study, treadmill-controlled, but large effects and near-zero risk.
The repeated-bout effect protects strengthNot supportedTen sessions over four weeks moved soreness 23.6 → 6.7 mm (p = 0.01) but knee-extensor strength loss −13.9% → −9.3%, n.s. throughout.
Hike above 10% gradientFolkloreCost per vertical metre is minimised at 20.4–35.0°; walking beats running at every angle ≥15.8°. The transition depends on grade and speed jointly. At 550–700 m/h, running a climb never pays.
The 10% rule for weekly volume increasesFalsifiedRCT, n = 532: no difference in injury incidence (~20% both arms). Defensible bound is under ~30% week-on-week.
ACWR predicts injuryDiscreditedRatio-data artefacts, arbitrary windows, no causal estimation. Null in the one ultra cohort that tested it. What did precede injury: loads dropping 16–24% then rebounding 15–37% — yo-yo loading, not absolute load.
Longest run = 70–80% of race durationNo study existsKoop has coached successful ultrarunners whose longest runs were 20–80% of race distance. Treated here as risk management at 55–70%.
Poles save energyMostly notVertical cost of transport −2.0 to −2.8% at 25–36°, nothing at 10–20°. But RPE −14.4 to −18.5%, and over a Snowdon ascent-and-descent with a daypack, damage was reduced "almost to the point of complete disappearance." Use them for damage and RPE, not economy. Score or feel

Resting HR is your most trustworthy number; readiness is a composite that weights sleep heavily and pins at 1 without distinguishing degrees. Multi-day figures — training status, HRV weekly baseline — are computed over windows and are reliable.

Then feel, with a calibration: your history is 17 km when told 13.7, and HR 152 when told 140. Trust feel when it says stop. Distrust it when it says push.

Non-negotiables
  • Three runs a week, every week — the thread through all three races
  • Nothing eccentric after 21 October
  • Two full weeks of recovery after Madeira, 1–14 November
  • Swim four times a week from 15 November, no gap over five days
  • Body mass must not trend down
  • Descend the first steep section slower than feels necessary
The two things most likely to decide the outcome

1. Whether you arrive at Madeira with legs that have absorbed enough eccentric loading to survive 2,003 m of descent. The cheapest large win is the five-minute weighted downhill walk, done early and repeated every two to three weeks.

2. Whether you eat enough during an eleven-week ramp at 56 kg. Neither of these is about how long your long run is. The race is roughly a 40–45 km flat-equivalent effort against a 17 km longest run — that gap, not the vertical, is the headline problem.
