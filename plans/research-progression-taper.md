# Long-Run Progression, Strength and Taper — Research Findings

## 1. Time on feet vs distance

**Why duration, not distance — this part is lab-verified.** Minetti et al. 2002 measured net
metabolic cost across -45% to +45%. Level running costs **3.40 ± 0.24 J/kg/m**; at +45% it is
**18.93 ± 1.74** — a **~5.6x swing in energy cost per metre travelled**. Walking is cheaper
than running above ~+15-20%. **One kilometre is not a fixed dose of anything on this terrain,
so distance is an invalid load unit. Duration and vertical metres are the defensible units.**
This is the strongest evidence in the whole brief.

### The "70-80% of race duration" heuristic is folklore

**There is no RCT, cohort study, or meta-analysis testing longest-run-as-fraction-of-race-
duration.** It's a transplant from marathon lore (32 km ≈ 75-80% of 42.2 km) with no ultra
evidence base. What practitioners actually say:

- **Koop/CTS:** has coached successful ultrarunners whose longest runs were **20% to 80%** of
  race distance — the range is so wide the heuristic carries no information. Sets a practical
  **4 h minimum** only because that's how long it takes to rehearse fuelling.
- **Science of Ultra (Bearden):** argues the opposite — **2.5-3.0 h is the productive
  ceiling**, benefits decay with a ~1 h rate constant, beyond ~3 h "more negative breakdown
  than added positive stress." **Cites no primary sources — expert opinion, not evidence.**
- **Uphill Athlete's** published 50K plan tops out around a 50-mile week.

**Verdict: treat 55-70% of expected race duration as a planning convention chosen for risk
management, not a validated target.** For a 5-6.5 h race that's a **3:30-4:00 peak session**,
which also lands inside the 2.5-4 h zone both camps can live with.

### Back-to-back long days — evidence base is essentially zero

**No controlled trial has compared B2B weekends against a single long day** for ultra
performance or injury.

- **Against (Koop):** "back-to-back long runs carry a far greater injury risk than back-to-back
  hard days, and produce comparatively little additional adaptation."
- **For (Roche and others):** Koop's model suits *advanced* athletes chasing marginal gains;
  B2B is the lower-intensity way to accumulate volume.
- **Mechanistic argument for B2B here:** the second day rehearses running on eccentrically
  damaged legs, which is the actual failure mode at Madeira.

**Who they suit:** >=2 years consistent running, established long run >=3 h, no bone-stress
history, races >=50 km. **This athlete is none of those. Recommendation: no true
back-to-backs.** At most one "long + short" weekend near peak: 3:20 Saturday, 45-60 min very
easy Sunday.

## 2. Safe progression rate

### The 10% rule is not supported — this is settled

- **Buist et al. 2008 (GRONORUN, AJSM), RCT, n = 532 novice runners.** A 13-week graded
  programme built on the 10% rule vs a standard 8-week programme. **No difference in injury
  incidence (~20% in both arms).** Best-designed test of the rule; negative.
- **Nielsen et al. 2014 (JOSPT), prospective, n = 874.** **>30% vs <10% weekly progression:
  HR = 1.59 (95% CI 0.96-2.66)** — right direction, CI crosses 1. Injuries loading onto >30%:
  patellofemoral pain, ITBS, medial tibial stress syndrome, patellar tendinopathy,
  trochanteric bursitis, glute med/TFL. **Critically, 10-30% progression showed no elevation
  over <10%** (HR = 0.8, 95% CI 0.6-1.3).
- **Damsted et al. 2018 systematic review (IJSPT):** "**Very limited evidence** exists
  supporting that a sudden change in training load is associated with increased risk." The
  10% rule "lacks empirical support."

**What the evidence supports: stay under ~30% week-on-week, not 10%. Anything in 10-25% has
no signal of elevated risk.**

### ACWR — descriptive flag, not a rule

Impellizzeri et al. 2020 (IJSPP 15(6):907) demolish it on: **ratio-data artefacts** (numerator
contained in denominator → mathematical coupling and spurious correlation); **arbitrary time
windows** (why 7:28?); **no causal estimation** in any study; huge heterogeneity in the load
variable; no conceptual framework. **Do not build a plan around ACWR targets.**

### Trail-specific data

- **Coelho et al. 2020 (IJERPH), 25 male trail runners, 52 weeks, 38 injuries.** In the injury
  week vs preceding: sRPE load **1,103 → 1,379 AU**, training strain **913 → 1,359 AU**.
  The most useful finding is the *shape*: loads **dropped 16-24% from week -3 to week -2, then
  rebounded 15-37% into the injury week. Yo-yo loading, not absolute load, preceded injury.**
- **Comrades GAM study 2025 (n = 106, 43 injured).** **Inverse** load-injury relationship:
  relative risk **2.3 at <25 km/week, 1.0 at ~100 km/week, 0.8 at ~150 km/week**; frequency
  RR ~**3.0 at <2 sessions/week, 0.25 at 8**. **ACWR had no significant effect (p = 0.3).**
  Conclusion: "insufficient training may not prepare the runners for the demands of the
  ultradistance race." Retrospective/cross-sectional — directional only.

**The tension: ramping too fast risks tissue failure; ramping too slow leaves him unprepared
for 2,003 m of descent, which is its own injury mechanism. Resolution: smooth, monotonic
progression with no yo-yo, front-loaded on vertical rather than distance.**

### Concrete 11-week long-session progression

| Wk | Mon start | Long session (time on feet) | Flat-equiv km | Vert in session | Weekly vert | Δ vs prev peak |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Aug 17 | **1:50** | ~18-19 | 300 m | 500-700 m | baseline |
| 2 | Aug 24 | **2:05** | ~21 | 500 m | 900-1,100 m | +14% |
| 3 | Aug 31 | **2:20** | ~23 | 700 m | 1,200-1,500 m | +12% |
| 4 | Sep 7 | **1:30** (cutback) | ~15 | 400 m | ~700 m | -36% |
| 5 | Sep 14 | **2:35** | ~25 | 900 m | 1,600-1,900 m | +11% |
| 6 | Sep 21 | **2:55** | ~28 | 1,100 m | 2,000-2,300 m | +13% |
| 7 | Sep 28 | **2:00** (cutback) | ~20 | 600 m | ~1,200 m | -31% |
| 8 | Oct 5 | **3:20** (+ optional 50 min Sun) | ~32 | 1,400 m | 2,600-3,000 m | +14% |
| 9 | Oct 12 | **3:45-4:00 PEAK** (Sat Oct 17) | ~36 | **1,600-1,800 m** | 3,000-3,500 m | +15% |

Every increase is **11-15%** — inside the zone Nielsen found no risk signal in, less than half
the >30% threshold. No yo-yo pattern.

**Peak long run: Sat 17 October — 14-15 days out.** 3:45-4:00 = ~60-70% of expected race
duration and, more importantly, **80-90% of race vertical. Vertical specificity is the lever
that matters, not duration.**

**Caveat stated plainly:** with a 17 km starting long run and 11 weeks, the long session ends
up at ~45-50% of weekly running time by weeks 8-9, above the conventional 30-35% guard. That
guard is itself folklore, but the risk is real. Mitigate by keeping **frequency at 5-6
sessions/week** (Comrades data show frequency is protective) and putting a disproportionate
share of vertical into **low-impact modes** (stair machine, treadmill incline hiking).

## 3. Injury risk — 56 kg, BMI 21.9, 25M, rapid ramp

**Bone stress injury is the top-ranked risk**, ahead of tendinopathy.

- **Tenforde et al. 2015/2018:** low-BMD risk is **cumulative and steep**. 0-1 risk factors:
  **11.1%** had low BMD; 2 factors: **42.9%**; 3-4 factors: **80.0%**. Factor set: <85%
  expected body weight, >30 miles (48 km)/week, prior stress fracture, <1 serving/day
  calcium-rich food. Strongest combination OR = 17.3 (95% CI 1.6-185.6) — **absurdly wide CI,
  small study.**
- **Prior BSI raises subsequent BSI risk ~5-fold** — the strongest single predictor.

**RED-S in males — better evidenced than most assume:**
- **~25%** clinical LEA prevalence in elite male middle/long-distance athletes.
- In highly trained male endurance athletes, **76.9% had EA <30 kcal/kg FFM/day**, with
  elevated β-CTX and P1NP:β-CTX <100 (net bone resorption).
- **Randomised, in males:** 4 days at **30 kcal/kg FFM/day** vs 45 → **decreased P1NP,
  increased CTX-I**. A high-protein diet did **not** rescue it. Causal, in men, within days.

**Concrete targets.** At 56 kg / ~10% fat, FFM ≈ 50 kg:
- **LEA threshold: <1,500 kcal/day above exercise expenditure. Never go there.**
- **Target >=45 kcal/kg FFM/day = >=2,250 kcal/day *plus* exercise energy expenditure.**
- Rest day ≈ **2,300-2,600 kcal**. Peak-week day (4 h at 600-750 kcal/h) ≈ **4,800-5,000
  kcal**. **Under-fuelling the peak weekend is the most likely route to a stress fracture.**
- **Non-negotiable: body mass must not trend down across the 11 weeks.**

### Load monitoring with evidence behind it

| Metric | Evidence | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Session-RPE** = RPE x minutes | Validated vs HR-TRIMP and lactate | Log every session |
| **Monotony** = mean daily load / SD | Linked to overtraining with high load | Keep **<2.0** |
| **Strain** = weekly load x monotony | 913 → 1,359 AU in trail injury weeks | Flag >30% jumps |
| **Week-to-week *pattern*** | Dip 16-24% then rebound 15-37% preceded injury | **Avoid unplanned drops followed by catch-up weeks — most actionable finding** |
| ACWR | Discredited; null in ultra runners | Descriptive only |

**Stop-running warning signs (not "monitor"):** focal point-tender bone pain (tibia, femoral
neck/groin, navicular, metatarsal, sacrum) worsening with impact and persisting after warm-up
— **groin/hip pain on load is femoral neck until proven otherwise**; pain worse at the *end*
of a run; night pain or pain on single-leg hop; male RED-S proxies (loss of morning erections,
falling libido, repeated illness, motivation collapse).

**Base rates:** 19.6 injuries/1000 h and 12.3% prevalence in a 30-week trail cohort (n=152);
lower limb 82.9% of injuries — knee 29.8%, shin 18.0%, foot 13.7%. Note **training for
*shorter* ultra-trail races produced a higher injury rate** than longer ones (Mac Ultra 2022)
— a 25K is exactly the distance where people ramp hard and race hard.

## 4. Concurrent strength training

**Well supported — one of the better-evidenced interventions available.**

- **Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024 (Sports Medicine), 38 studies, 894 participants.** Running
  performance: **high load (>=80% 1RM) ES = -0.469 (p = 0.029)**; **combined methods
  ES = -1.035 (p = 0.036)**; **plyometric alone ES = -0.210 (p = 0.064, n.s.)**. **No
  significant effect on VO2max, vVO2max or MMSS (all p > 0.479)** — the benefit is
  **neuromuscular/economy, not metabolic**.
- **Llanos-Lagos et al. 2023, on running economy:** **high load ES = -0.266 (p = 0.039)**;
  **combined ES = -0.426 (p = 0.018)**; plyometrics no main effect **but effective at
  <=12 km/h (ES = -0.307, p = 0.028)** — exactly the speed band he'll race at. Submaximal load
  and isometric: no effect.
- **Balsalobre-Fernández et al. 2016 (JSCR), 5 studies, n = 93 highly trained:** large
  beneficial effect on RE. What worked: **resistance + plyometrics, 2-3x/week for 8-12 weeks**,
  ~3:1 endurance:strength ratio, **not to failure**.
- **Beattie et al. 2017 (JSCR), 40-week controlled trial, n = 20:** significant improvement in
  maximal strength, reactive strength, running economy and vVO2max at 20 and 40 weeks;
  controls changed on nothing; **no hypertrophy, no body-composition change**.
- **Rønnestad & Mujika 2014:** RE improves with **either heavy or explosive** work;
  **two sessions/week** typically sufficient.

### Prescription (weeks 1-8)

| Element | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Frequency | **2x/week** |
| Heavy block | **3-5 sets x 3-6 reps at >=80% 1RM**, 2-3 min rest, **never to failure**. Back squat or trap-bar deadlift + Bulgarian split squat + single-leg calf raise |
| Eccentric/descent-specific | **Slow-eccentric step-downs (3-4 s lowering), 3 x 8-10/leg**; Nordic hamstring 3 x 5 |
| Plyometrics | Weeks 3-8 only: **2-4 sets x 5-10 reps**, bodyweight, 1.5-3 min rest |
| Uphill-specific | Weighted step-ups, loaded carries, hip abduction/ER (knee = 30% of trail injuries) |
| Progression | **Add load, not reps.** Keep total set count flat |

### Sequencing to minimise interference

- **Separate strength and running by >=3 h.** AMPK stays elevated >=3 h after high-intensity
  endurance work and blunts the mTOR signalling strength training depends on.
- **Doma et al.: running economy is degraded for up to 8 h after lower-limb strength work.**
  Never put strength within 24 h *before* a key run; schedule on easy-run days or after the
  run, and **>=48 h before the weekly long session**.
- Running interferes with strength more than cycling does. **Accept that strength gains will be
  modest in an 11-week race block — the goal is preservation and descent-armour, not PRs.**

### Final weeks

- **Weeks 9-10:** reduce to **1-2 sessions/week, cut volume ~50%, keep load >=80% 1RM.**
  Intensity is what preserves the adaptation.
- **Last heavy lower-body session: 10-14 days out (~Oct 18-21).**
- **Last plyometric / eccentric-heavy / downhill session: 14 days out.**
- **Race week:** at most one neural-priming session >=5 days out — **2 x 3-5 fast reps at
  moderate load**, familiar movements, zero novelty, zero soreness. Then cessation. >=2 days
  cessation optimises maximal strength expression; beyond ~6-7 days starts to cost.

## 5. Taper

**Robust core finding.**

- **Bosquet et al. 2007 (MSSE), meta-analysis, 27 of 182 studies.** Optimal: **2-week taper,
  volume reduced 41-60% exponentially, with intensity and frequency UNCHANGED.** Overall
  effect small (ES ~0.2) → **~2-3% performance improvement**.
- **PLOS One 2023 meta-analysis (14 studies, 174 athletes):** time trial improved pre-to-post
  **SMD = -0.45**, vs control **SMD = -0.73 (95% CI -1.25 to -0.21)**; TTE **SMD = 1.28**.
  **Largest effect at 8-14 days (SMD = -1.47). 41-60% volume cut beat both <=40% and >=60%.
  Progressive taper beat step taper (SMD -0.51, p < 0.05).** Maintaining intensity and
  frequency was necessary. **VO2max and running economy did not change** — the gain is fatigue
  removal, not fitness.
- **Pre-taper overload** produced significantly better outcomes than conventional taper
  (p < 0.05). The week-9 peak weekend *is* the overload.
- **Strava marathon data (n = 158,117):** strict 3-week tapers gave the largest benefit,
  **median 5:32 = 2.6%**. **Observational; authors explicitly disclaim causation.**
  Self-selection is a serious confound.

**Ultra vs marathon: there is no evidence.** No study has compared taper protocols in ultra or
mountain runners. "Ultrarunners need longer tapers" is **coaching consensus, not data**.
Practitioner convergence: **10-21 days, 14 as default**; CTS states there's no benefit below
10-14 or above 21 days. Mechanistically there *is* a defensible reason to lean long here —
eccentric damage from the peak session resolves slower than glycogen — but that's inference.

**Recommendation: strict, progressive, 14-15 day taper, volume -50% then -70%, intensity and
frequency maintained.**

### Week by week

**Week 9 — peak/overload (Oct 12-18).** Peak long session **Sat Oct 17, 3:45-4:00,
1,600-1,800 m vert**, full dress rehearsal. Sunday: 30-40 min walk/jog or rest. **Last session
of the training block.**

**Week 10 — Taper 1 (Oct 19-25). Volume -45 to -55%. Frequency unchanged. Intensity
maintained.**
- Mon rest or 30 min very easy
- Tue 50 min with **5 x 3 min at race-uphill effort** on 10-12% incline, full recovery
- Wed 40 min easy
- Thu 45 min with **6 x 1 min uphill** + 4 x 20 s strides
- Fri rest
- **Sat Oct 24: last long session — 2:00, 600-800 m vert**, race kit, race shoes, ~20 min
  downhill. Nothing hard.
- Sun 30-40 min easy or off
- Strength: one session Mon/Tue, half volume, load maintained. **Nothing eccentric after
  Oct 21.**

**Week 11 — race week (Oct 26 - Nov 1). Volume -70 to -75%. Keep frequency and short bouts of
race intensity.**
- Mon rest
- Tue **45 min: 4 x 90 s at race-climb effort** + 4 x 20 s strides. **Do not skip this**
- Wed 30 min easy
- Thu 30 min easy + 4 x 20 s strides. Travel
- Fri 20-25 min very easy on Madeira terrain — surface and grip recon, not a workout
- Sat 20 min shakeout + 3 x 20 s strides, kit check, carb load
- **Sun Nov 1: RACE**

**Carb loading:** 8-12 g/kg/day for 36-48 h = **450-670 g/day** at 56 kg. Low fibre final 24 h.

**Expected gain ~2-3%** — on a 6 h race, **7-11 minutes**. Worth having, not worth obsessing
over; the taper's larger value here is removing eccentric damage before a race that inflicts
far more.

## 6. Race-specific prep

**Allocation.** Weeks 1-4 general; weeks 5-9 progressively specific — by week 9 **~70-80% of
the long session should be race-like** (sustained climbing, technical descent, race kit, race
fuelling, race time of day). Weeks 10-11: specificity preserved, volume not.

### Heat acclimation — skip it

- Madeira November: **9-21 degC** per the official event page (Funchal averages ~20-21 degC day /
  15-17 degC night, ~71% humidity, ~63 mm rain over ~11 days). Mountain interior cooler and
  wetter.
- **Meta-analysis (35 studies):** HA gives TTE ES 0.86, TT ES 0.49, VO2max ES 0.30. **These
  gains occur in hot environments; testing in thermoneutral (<25 degC) was minimal.**
- Protocol if pursued: 60-120 min exposures, >=5 days for HR/Tcore, 10 days for power. Decay
  ~2.5%/day; re-acclimation 8-12x faster than decay.
- Scoon 2007: **~13 sauna sessions over 3 weeks, 30 min at ~90 degC post-run → plasma volume
  +7.1%, TTE +32%. n = 6.**
- **Verdict: at 9-21 degC the heat stimulus is marginal. Low-confidence, low-yield. Not
  recommended.** The opposite risk — **hypothermia at 9 degC in rain on exposed ridges during a
  5-6 h day at 56 kg** — deserves more attention than heat does.

### Gut training — do this; best cost:benefit in this section

- Single-transportable carbohydrate saturates at **~60 g/h**; glucose + fructose (SGLT1 +
  GLUT5) raises capacity to **75-90 g/h**.
- **Costa et al. 2019 (IJSNEM), the ultramarathon nutrition position paper:** the 90 g/h figure
  "**probably requires scaling down for ultramarathon activities, due to the lower absolute
  exercise intensity**." **GI symptom prevalence after ultramarathon: 60-96%.**
- Contrary data: 90 vs 76 g/h produced **greater symptom severity and feeding intolerance**.
  Real-world ultrarunners average ~**37 g/h**.
- **Prescription: target 60-80 g/h.** Start at 40-50 g/h in week 3, add ~10 g/h every two
  weeks, reach race rate by week 8 — **~7-8 rehearsal sessions**. Exact race products. Fluid
  **400-800 ml/h**; sodium ~**500-800 mg/h** (evidence for specific sodium numbers is weak —
  individualise).

### Kit — all raced-in by the Oct 17 peak session

- **Trail shoes with aggressive, soft-compound outsole.** Madeira's **basalt is notoriously
  slippery when wet** and levada trails are frequently muddy. Single most consequential
  equipment decision.
- Pack with full mandatory kit weight (**kit checks are enforced at Madeira — see race facts**).
- Waterproof shell (11 rain days/month in November), warm layer, anti-chafe, socks, headtorch.
- **Toenails trimmed, adequate toe-box length** — 2,003 m of descent destroys toenails in
  undersized shoes.

### Poles

Legal and worth using for 2,003 m of climb, but **a motor skill** — introduce by week 3, use in
every long session from week 5. Untrained pole use wastes energy and shreds hands/shoulders.
The least pole-experienced participant in Giovanelli's field study was the one who got slower.

## Evidence ledger

**Strong:**
- Gradient-dependent energy cost (Minetti 2002)
- Taper: 2 weeks, -41 to -60% volume, intensity/frequency maintained, ~2-3% gain
- The 10% rule does **not** prevent injury (Buist 2008 RCT)
- Strength training improves economy/performance via neuromuscular routes, not VO2max
- LEA at 30 kcal/kg FFM/day suppresses bone formation in men within days (RCT)

**Weak / contested / observational:**
- ACWR — methodologically discredited, null in the one ultra cohort that tested it
- >30% weekly progression as a threshold — HR 1.59 with CI crossing 1
- The 3-week marathon taper advantage — observational, heavy self-selection
- 90 g/h carbohydrate — explicitly flagged as probably too high for ultra intensities

**Pure folklore — flag as such:**
- **"Longest run = 70-80% of race duration."** No study exists. Koop's own range is 20-80%
- **Back-to-back long runs.** Zero controlled evidence either way
- **"Ultrarunners need a longer taper."** Plausible, untested
- **"100 m ascent = 1 km flat."** Convenient, unvalidated
- **The 3-hour long-run ceiling and its "1-hour rate constant."** Confidently stated, uncited

**The two things most likely to determine the outcome:** (1) whether he arrives with legs that
have absorbed enough eccentric loading to survive 2,003 m of descent, and (2) whether he eats
enough during an 11-week ramp at 56 kg. **Neither is about the long run's length.**
