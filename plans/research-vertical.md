# Vertical / Climbing — Research Findings

## 1. When to hike vs run — the gradient question

### The lab data

**Minetti et al. 2002** (J Appl Physiol, n=10, treadmill -45% to +45%) — the foundational
dataset. Energy cost in J/kg/m:

| Gradient | Walking | Running |
|---|---|---|
| Level | 1.64 ± 0.50 (min, ~1.0 m/s) | 3.40 ± 0.24 (speed-independent) |
| -10% | **0.81 ± 0.37** (walking min) | — |
| -20% | — | **1.73 ± 0.36** (running min) |
| +45% | **17.33 ± 1.11** | 18.93 ± 1.74 |
| -45% | 3.46 ± 0.95 | 3.92 ± 0.81 |

At +45% walking is **8.5% cheaper** than running. Above +15% the elastic stretch-shortening
cycle that makes level running cheap **disappears** — efficiency converges on pure concentric
muscle efficiency (~25%). That is the mechanism, not a preference.

**Minetti 2003:** the *critical slope* above which walking is cheapest is **~13-15%**.

**Giovanelli, Ortiz, Henninger & Kram 2016** ("is steeper cheaper?", n=15, fixed vertical
velocity 0.35 m/s): cost **per vertical metre** is minimised between **20.4° and 35.0°
(37-70% grade)**. Walking beat running at **every angle >=15.8°**.

**Ortiz et al. 2017** (30° incline): walking **20.6% cheaper at 0.3 m/s**, only **2.8% cheaper
at 0.8 m/s**, no different at ~0.8-0.9 m/s. The gaits converge; above that running wins.

**Lemire et al. 2021** (Front Physiol, 8 slopes) — **the single most important finding for a
flatlander**: economy at every slope correlated with level economy **except +20%**
(p >= 0.214). **Steep uphill economy is a separate, trainable trait that cannot be inferred
from flat running ability.** Oxygen cost (mlO2/kg/km): 0% = 39.9, +5% = 47.1, +10% = 53.6,
+15% = 58.2, +20% = 67.5.

### The crossover in the units that matter

Converting each crossover to vertical ascent rate:

| Gradient | Crossover speed | Vertical rate |
|---|---|---|
| 10° / 18% | 2.14 m/s | ~1,340 m/h |
| 20° / 36% | 1.32 m/s | ~1,625 m/h |
| 30° / 58% | 0.8-0.9 m/s | ~1,440-1,620 m/h |

**The crossover sits at ~1,300-1,600 m/h across the whole 10-30° range.** Elite mountain
runners (VO2max 67.6) sustain **1,409 m/h** all-out and **1,110 m/h** at ultra intensity.

**Conclusion: at an estimated 550-700 m/h, there is no gradient in this race where running
the climbs is economical for him. Hike every sustained climb above ~15%.** Run only the
sub-10% links and flats.

### Where folklore diverges

- **"Hike above 10% grade"** — Koop explicitly rejects it; the transition depends on
  **grade x speed jointly**, and the lab data agree. Too conservative for slow runners,
  too aggressive for fast ones.
- **The heuristic that survives:** hike when hiking pace equals running pace at the same
  RPE. Equivalently — **if you're not climbing faster than ~1,300 m/h, hike.**
- **"Steeper is cheaper"** is real but bounded — only per vertical metre, only 20-35°, only
  at fixed vertical velocity. It does not mean steep climbs feel easier.

## 2. Poles and technique

### Poles — quantified, and more modest than the hype

**Giovanelli et al. 2019** (n=14, treadmill, 10.1° to 38.9°):
- Vertical cost of transport: **-2.55% at 25.4°, -2.79% at 29.8°, -2.00% at 35.5%**
  (p < 0.05). **No significant saving at 10.1°, 15.5°, 19.8° or 38.9°.**
- **RPE reduced far more than energy: -14.4% to -18.5%** (p < 0.01).
- Stride frequency **-6.7%**, stride length **+8.6%**.
- **Pole length 58 ± 2% of body height** for steep uphill → **~93 cm for a 160 cm athlete**
  (range 88-100 cm). Note this is *shorter* than standard trekking-pole fitting.

**Giovanelli et al. 2022** (field study, 1.3 km / 433 m D+, mean 19.5°) — the most externally
valid:
- Maximal effort: **18:51 with poles vs 19:19 without (p = .013), 12/15 improved, mean gain
  2.6%.** But **effect size d = 0.08 — "small".**
- **At 80% intensity (i.e. ultra pace) literally nothing differed**: time 23:56 vs 23:57,
  VO2 46.1 vs 46.1, HR 150.5 vs 150.5, RPE 11.5 vs 11.9 — all n.s.
- The two fastest athletes were **slower** with poles (they could run the shallower sections).

**Howatson et al. 2011** (MSSE, n=37, Mount Snowdon ascent **and descent** with daypack) —
**this is the finding that justifies poles for this race**: the pole group showed attenuated
loss of MVC immediately, at 24 h and 48 h; **significantly lower soreness at 24 and 48 h;
lower CK at 24 h.** Howatson: damage reduced "almost to the point of complete disappearance."

**Contradictory earlier work:** Knight & Caldwell 2000, Foissac 2008 — poles reduce lower-limb
EMG and increase upper-limb EMG, but at shallow grades (8.5-12°) **increase VO2 by ~7%**;
Nordic-walking studies report 20-40% higher cost. Metabolic crossover somewhere in **15-26%
grade**. Below that, poles cost energy.

**Verdict:** the case for poles here rests on **RPE reduction (-15 to -18%)** and
**muscle-damage protection**, not metabolic saving. Both matter enormously for a 17 km-max
athlete facing 2,003 m each way. **Critical caveat (Koop): there is no published research on
pole use while *running*, and biomechanics change substantially — train with them for weeks,
not days.** Confirm they're permitted at Madeira.

### Technique — mostly untested

| Element | Prescription | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Hands-on-quads | Palm on thigh above knee, push down/back as that leg extends | **No metabolic study found.** Universally coached, mechanically plausible, **untested folklore** |
| Cadence | Short quick constant steps | Indirect only; optimal hiking cadence untested |
| Stride length | Shorten | **Conflicts with poles**, which lengthen stride +8.6% and drop frequency -6.7%. Unresolved |
| Torso angle | Lean **from the ankles**, not hunched at the hip | Indirect: propulsive GRF rises ~75% at +9° vs level |
| Foot placement | Whole-foot contact, let the heel drop | Weak; coaching consensus only |

**Muscle recruitment shift** (uphill vs level): **vasti +23%, soleus +14%**, semitendinosus
-17%, gracilis -18%, **rectus femoris -29%**. Glute max approaches maximal isometric capacity
at steep grades. **Train glutes / vasti / soleus.**

## 3. Training vertical on flat terrain

### First — "no hills within 2 hours" is not accurate

Reachable from Amsterdam:

| Place | Travel | Relief |
|---|---|---|
| **Zuid-Kennemerland dunes** (Overveen/Bloemendaal) | 20-25 min train | 30-50 m; Kopje van Bloemendaal 50 m |
| **Schoorl "Klimduin"** | ~1 h | **~50 m of very steep sand**, repeatable |
| **Utrechtse Heuvelrug** (Amerongse Berg) | ~1 h | ~69 m |
| **Posbank / Veluwezoom** | ~1 h15-1 h30 | **up to ~90-110 m, genuinely runnable** — best real terrain in NL |
| **Berg en Dal / Nijmegen** | ~1 h20 | 60-80 m |

**A day at Posbank doing 20+ reps of a 90 m climb = ~1,800 m of real ascent *and descent*.**
No machine provides the descent. **Recommend 2-3 dedicated day trips during the build**, one
as a full race simulation with pack and poles.

### Vertical rate reference tables

**Treadmill** (most cap at 15% = 8.53°, well below the 20-35° optimum — you get the vertical
but **not** the joint angles or force magnitudes):

| Grade | 4.5 km/h | 5.0 | 5.5 | 6.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10% | 447 m/h | 497 | 547 | 597 |
| 12% | 535 | 595 | 655 | 714 |
| **15%** | **668** | **742** | **816** | **890** |
| 20% | 883 | 981 | 1,079 | 1,177 |

**Stairs** (Dutch riser 17-18 cm, tread 27-29 cm → pitch **~30-33°, squarely inside the
20-35° metabolic optimum**):

| Step rate | Vertical rate |
|---|---|
| 60/min | 612 m/h |
| 70/min | 714 m/h |
| 80/min | 816 m/h |
| 100/min | 1,020 m/h |

**Stairs are the best flat-city substitute** — correct pitch, correct hip/knee flexion, and
uniquely they also give **eccentric descent**. A 15-floor tower ≈ 45 m per ascent.
**Madeira's course contains long stone staircases**, so the specificity is real.

**StairMaster / step mill:** effective angle 70-100%, closest machine to race pitch.
Practitioner consensus (Evoke Endurance, Uphill Athlete) is it beats a 15% treadmill —
**no head-to-head study exists.**

**Parking garages:** ramps 12-17%, ~15-20 m per lap over 6 levels. Continuous, poor angle.

### Weekly vertical volume target

Coaching consensus (Vert.run, Uphill Athlete): peak weekly vertical of **50-80% of race D+**.
**This is convention, not evidence — no trial has tested it.**

For 2,003 m D+: **peak week 1,000-1,600 m**, with one long session of **1,000-1,400 m in a
single push**. Given his low running base, pushing vertical via hiking/stairs is *safer* than
pushing running mileage.

### Session templates

**Vert grind (aerobic muscular endurance) — 1-2x/week.** Treadmill 12-15% at 5.0-5.5 km/h,
**hands off the rails**, or step mill at RPE 5-6. 45-75 min continuous → **600-1,000 m**.
Load progression: bodyweight → 5% BW (2.8 kg) → 8% (4.5 kg) → 10% (5.6 kg) → 15% (8.4 kg).
**Progress load OR duration, never both in the same week.** Cue: "legs burning, still able to
talk." **HR is unreliable here** — the limitation is muscular, not cardiac.

**Stairwell repeats — 1x/week.** Tower >=15 floors (~45 m/rep), hands on quads, no handrail.
8 reps (360 m) → 12 (540) → 16 (720) → 20 (900) → 24 (1,080). **Walk down at least half the
reps** — that's the cheap eccentric stimulus.

**Long race-simulation brick — every 2-3 weeks, building to 3.5-4.5 h.** Alternate 20-25 min
climbing (treadmill/step mill/stairs) with 15-20 min flat running outside, 5-8x. **The only
session that rehearses the actual sawtooth demand on tired legs.** Full kit.

## 4. Uphill physiology and intervals

### What limits sustained climbing

**Bascuas et al. 2024** (VK 4.64 km / 835 m D+, n=15): "**peripheral limitations may play a
more significant role than central limitations**." Quadriceps muscle-oxygenation correlated
with performance (r = 0.54 mean; r = -0.76 for coefficient of variation). Cardiovascular
factors showed minimal impact at submaximal intensity. *(n=15, correlational, single lab.)*

**Fatigue shows up as loss of vertical velocity:** across sections of the same climb,
mechanical power at 20° fell 3.38 → 2.46 W/kg; vertical velocity at 15° fell 0.35 → 0.26 m/s
(**-26%**).

**Zimmermann et al. 2022** (n=14, 375 m hill, mean 22.3%): running vs walking uphill gave
**statistically identical VO2max (55.2 vs 54.4), peak HR (175 vs 176), lactate (8.7 vs 9.1)
and cost of locomotion (6.87 vs 7.02 J/kg/m)**. Running was faster but only for athletes able
to run that grade — not applicable here.

**Practical diagnostic** (Uphill Athlete, mechanistically sound, not formally validated): if
HR *fails to reach* what your RPE says it should on a steep loaded climb, your **legs**, not
your heart, are the limiter.

### Interval protocols

| Protocol | Prescription | Evidence quality |
|---|---|---|
| **Long uphill HIIT** | **2 x 4 min at 10% grade, 90-95% HRmax, 2 min active recovery; progress to 5 min from week 7.** 2x/week | Panthong 2026, masters trail runners: 18 km TT 281 → 225 min, VO2max 40.3 → 49.2, uphill O2 cost -14%. **Effect sizes implausibly large; low-trained cohort. Upper bound, not expectation** |
| **Short uphill HIIT** | **10+ x 30 s steep near-maximal, 2-3 min recovery, 2x/week, 6 weeks** | Ferley 2013 (n=32 well-trained): **level-grade produced *greater* Tmax gains.** Uphill HIIT is **not** clearly superior — its value is specificity and lower impact load |
| **30/30s** | 30 s at 92-95% HRmax / 30 s easy. 1-2 sets x 8-10 min → build to 1 x 30 min | Billat; well established for vVO2max, not uphill-specific |
| **VK-style sustained** | 15-30 min continuous at RCP intensity on 15%+ treadmill or stairs | Modelled on Giovanelli 2022 field data |
| **Muscular endurance (ME)** | Weighted vest, **30-100% grade**, Zone 3-4, 1x/week, block of **6-10 workouts over 6-8 weeks**, >=3 easy days after | Uphill Athlete method. Coherent, widely used, **no published RCT** |

**Intensity distribution: ~80-85% easy / 15-20% hard, with nearly all the hard work on an
incline.** Not because uphill intervals are magically better (Ferley says they aren't) but
because (a) uphill economy is a separate trait only uphill work develops, and (b) incline work
delivers high metabolic load at low impact load — which suits an athlete whose
musculoskeletal durability is the binding constraint.

## 5. Estimating time and effort

### VAM benchmarks

| Level | Sustained VAM |
|---|---|
| Beginner / recreational hiker | 300-450 m/h |
| Fit recreational trail runner | 450-700 m/h |
| Well-trained amateur | 700-1,000 m/h |
| Elite at ultra intensity | 1,110 m/h |
| Elite, ~19-min field effort | 1,375-1,409 m/h |
| Elite, lab vvert max | 1,827 ± 233 m/h |

**Realistic target for this athlete after 11 weeks: 550-700 m/h.**

### Classical rules

- **Naismith (1892):** 1 h/5 km + 1 h/600 m ascent → **8.3 h**. No descent penalty at all.
- **Scarf's equivalence:** 1 m ascent ≡ **7.92 m** horizontal (~8:1) → 2,003 m ≡ **15.9 km**
  → **40.9 km flat-equivalent**. **The best-documented figure.**
- **Langmuir (1984):** base 5 km/h; **+10 min per 300 m of steep (>12°) descent** → ~9.4 h.
  **The only classical rule that penalises steep descent — the most appropriate one here.**
- **Tobler's hiking function:** implies a constant **~460-505 m/h** across steep grades.
  Describes a hiker, not a trail runner.

### Grade-adjusted pace and its failure modes

Minetti polynomial (basis of Strava GAP), applied as a **sawtooth** (6.68 km climbing at 30%,
6.68 km descending at 30%, 11.6 km rolling):
- Climb: C(0.30) = 12.58 J/kg/m → 3.49x flat → 6.68 km ≡ **23.3 km**
- Descent: C(-0.30) = 2.46 J/kg/m → 0.68x flat → 6.68 km ≡ **4.6 km**
- Flat: **11.6 km**
- **Total ≈ 39.5 km flat-equivalent**

Smearing the ascent evenly instead gives 33.1 km — **a ~20% difference. Use the sawtooth;
it's what Madeira actually is.**

**Known failures of GAP, worth stating out loud:**
1. Fitted from **treadmill running only** (n=10, smooth belt). No walking data. Extrapolating
   it to power hiking is unjustified.
2. **Over-credits descent.** Strava's own engineering found the real downhill benefit peaks
   near **-10%** and reverses beyond; they re-fit against user HR data rather than Minetti.
3. Ignores **technical footing, eccentric damage and cumulative fatigue** — the three things
   that will actually decide this race.

### Flat-equivalent heuristics compared

| Rule | Result | Support |
|---|---|---|
| "100 m ascent = 1 km flat" | 45 km | Most-cited. **No primary source. Folklore** |
| "100 m = 800 m flat" | 41 km | Weak; race-result regressions |
| **Scarf/Naismith 7.92:1** | **40.9 km** | **Best documented** |
| Minetti/GAP sawtooth | 39.5 km | Strongest physiology, over-credits descent |

**Convergent answer: this 25 km race is a ~40-45 km flat-equivalent effort. His longest run
is 17 km. That gap — not the vertical per se — is the headline problem.**

### Time estimate

| Component | Assumption | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Ascent 2,003 m | 550-700 m/h | **2 h 50 - 3 h 40** |
| Descent 2,003 m | 900-1,200 m/h (steep, technical, novice) | **1 h 40 - 2 h 15** |
| Flat links + checkpoints | | **0 h 30 - 0 h 50** |
| **Total** | | **5 h 00 - 6 h 45** |

**Against an 11.5 h cut-off this is ~5 hours of margin. Timing out is not the risk; blowing
up is.**

### Fuelling implication

Sawtooth Minetti model at 56 kg: climb 1,124 kcal + descent 220 + flat 559 ≈ **1,900 kcal
net**, ~2,200-2,400 gross over ~6 h → **~380-420 kcal/h**. Target **60-90 g carbohydrate/h**
and rehearse it in the long bricks.

## Evidence ledger

**Strong / replicated across labs:**
- Minetti cost-vs-gradient curve; ~8.5% walking advantage at +45%
- Walking cheaper than running above ~15-16° (Minetti, Giovanelli, Ortiz — three datasets)
- Vertical cost minimised at 20-35°
- **Uphill economy at >=+20% is uncorrelated with level economy** (Lemire 2021)
- Poles reduce lower-limb EMG, increase upper-limb EMG, reduce joint forces
- Poles reduce eccentric damage on a real mountain ascent+descent (Howatson 2011, n=37)

**Moderate — real but small, single-lab, or small-n:**
- Poles save ~2-3% vertical CoT at 25-35° and ~2.6% on maximal climbs; **d = 0.08**, n=14-15,
  one lab, unblindable. **Poles do nothing measurable at submaximal intensity**
- Poles reduce RPE 14-19% — large and consistent, but subjective and unblindable
- Peripheral > central limitation in VK (n=15, correlational)

**Folklore — use, but don't cite as science:**
- **Hands-on-quads push:** no metabolic study exists
- **"100 m ascent = 1 km flat":** no primary source
- **"Hike above 10% grade":** contradicted by the speed-dependence in every dataset
- **"Weekly vert = 50-80% of race vert":** pure convention, zero trials
- **Weighted-vest ME protocols:** coherent theory, no published RCT
- **Optimal hiking cadence / foot placement / torso angle:** essentially untested
- **StairMaster vs incline treadmill:** no head-to-head study
- **Pole use while running:** no published research at all
