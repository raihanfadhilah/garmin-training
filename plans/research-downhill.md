# Downhill / Eccentric Training — Research Findings

## 0. Framing the demand

- **Mountain Index** (elevation gain ÷ distance, Vert.run) = **2003 / 25 = 80 m/km**. For
  reference a "mountain 50K" is typically ~60 m/km. This is an unusually dense course —
  the vert, not the distance, is the event.
- ~2,003 m of descent spread over roughly 10-12 km of downhill = a very large cumulative
  eccentric dose. For calibration, the standard *lab* damage protocol that reliably wrecks
  trained trail runners is **5 km at -15% = 750 m of elevation loss**
  ([Trail-runner exploratory study, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12846201/)).
  The race is **~2.7x that dose**, on technical ground, after already climbing 2,000 m.
- Knee-extensor strength loss during downhill running averages **-23.5% (untrained)** and
  **-16.4% (trained)**; in 24 h running events knee-extensor loss reaches **-40%**, ~75% of
  which is *central* (drive) rather than peripheral. Expect large, unavoidable strength loss —
  the goal of training is to shift the curve, not eliminate it.

## 1. The repeated-bout effect (RBE) — what it does and doesn't buy

**Magnitude.** A single prior eccentric bout reduces subsequent damage markers by roughly
**50-80%** (CK, soreness, strength loss), and the size of the protection scales with how
damaging the first bout was.

**Duration.** Contested, and the range is wide:
- Maximal eccentric contractions: protection reported **up to 6 months**.
- Low-intensity bouts: as little as **~3 weeks**.
- Practitioner consensus for running: **2-6 weeks**, hence "re-expose every 2-3 weeks".
- Source: [Solent brief review](https://pure.solent.ac.uk/en/publications/eccentric-exercise-exercise-induced-muscle-damage-and-the-repeate/),
  [Nosaka & Clarkson RBE review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12641640/).

**The important caveat — RBE protects soreness, not fatigue resistance.** In a 2025 study
using 10 downhill sessions over 4 weeks
([PMC11849091](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11849091/)):
- Quadriceps soreness fell from **23.6 ± 22.2 mm → 8.6 ± 7.0 mm after 5 bouts (n.s.)
  → 6.7 ± 8.1 mm after 10 bouts (p = 0.01)**. So it took **10 sessions**, not 1-2, to
  significantly blunt soreness.
- Knee-extensor strength loss did **not** improve: -13.9% at baseline, -10.4% after 5 bouts,
  -9.3% after 10 bouts, **no significant difference**. Central and peripheral fatigue
  responses were unchanged.

**Interpretation for the plan:** downhill exposure will make him *hurt less and recover
faster*, and habitual downhill work is associated with lower CK (182 vs 290 U/L) and better
squat-strength retention (+4% vs -9.1%) after a damaging descent
([PMC12846201](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12846201/)). But it will **not**
prevent his quads going soft late in the race. Race-day pacing on the descents matters as
much as training. This is the single most important honest caveat in this section.

## 2. The best-evidenced downhill running protocol

**Toigo/Kowalchuk group, 4-week downhill training study**
([PMC8927009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8927009/)) — the most transferable
protocol in the literature:

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Gradients | -5%, -10%, -15% run consecutively in one session |
| Speed | 8.4 / 10.3 / 11.8 / 13.0 km/h (level → -15%) |
| Intensity | 60-65% VO2max — *easy*, not hard |
| Warm-up | 7 min level + 3 min at -10% |
| Session length | **15 min, progressed to 30 min** |
| Frequency | **10 sessions over 4 weeks**, 1-2 days rest between |
| Progression | increase total time and/or time spent at -10% and -15% |

**Results at 4 weeks:** eccentric MVT **+15.2%** (+8.6% by week 2), isometric **+9.7%**,
concentric **+12.9%**; quad CSA **+7.1%**, volume **+6.6%**, pennation angle **+5.8%**,
fascicle length **+2.7%**; VL EMG **+35.6%**. **VO2max unchanged** — this is a *structural
and neural* intervention, not an aerobic one.

**Key point:** the intensity was low (60-65% VO2max). The stimulus is the gradient, not the
effort. This matters hugely for a runner with an 11-week window and low durability — the
downhill work is cheap aerobically and expensive structurally.

**Recovery timeline between sessions** (from
[EJAP 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11129977/)): after a damaging downhill
bout, force loss recovers by **day 3**, late-phase RFD by **day 4**, and CK / swelling /
soreness fully resolve by **day 4**. So 48 h is enough between *moderate* sessions;
**allow 3-4 days after a genuinely hard one.**

## 2b. THE KEY FINDING — a 5-minute downhill *walk* buys most of the protection

[Prevention of downhill walking-induced muscle damage by non-damaging downhill walking,
PLOS One 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5348007/) — the most directly
actionable study I found for a flatlander:

**Preconditioning bout:** **-28% gradient, 5 km/h, 5 minutes, 10% body-mass pack**
= approximately **110 m of vertical descent**. Walking, not running.

**Damaging bout one week later:** same gradient/speed/load, **40 minutes ≈ 900 m descent**.

**Protection conferred by that single 5-minute walk:**

| Marker | Control | Preconditioned | Attenuation |
|---|---|---|---|
| MVIC strength loss | **-19.2 ± 6.9%** | **-9.9 ± 9.6%** | **49%** |
| Peak CK | 635.5 ± 306.0 U/L | 339.3 ± 148.4 U/L | **47%** |
| Peak soreness (VAS) | 81.4 ± 14.8 mm | 27.8 ± 16.8 mm | **66%** |

**Why this matters so much here:**
- **-28% is almost exactly the race's steep descent gradient (25-30%).** The specificity is
  near-perfect, unlike the -10/-15% treadmill literature.
- **110 m of descent is achievable in Amsterdam.** ~3 laps of a 40 m dune face, or roughly
  **37 flights** of a stairwell, or a handful of car-park ramp descents.
- **It's walking** — low injury risk, low aerobic cost, safe to give a STRAINED athlete.
- **10% body mass = ~5.6 kg for him**, which is realistic race-pack weight anyway.
- Crucially this one **did** attenuate **strength loss** (49%), which the 10-session running
  study did *not*. The steeper gradient and added load are the likely reason — this is a
  *higher-force, lower-volume* stimulus.
- It directly undercuts Koop's blanket dismissal of stairs and weighted vests for descent
  prep. A loaded, steep, *walked* descent is a well-evidenced protocol; his objection is
  aimed at weighted *running*, which is a different thing.

**Caveat:** single study, healthy young subjects, treadmill-controlled, damage markers rather
than race performance. But the effect sizes are large and the protocol is cheap and
low-risk — high expected value even discounted for uncertainty.

Also relevant: downhill *walking* causes real muscle damage regardless of intensity, and
markedly more next-day soreness than uphill walking. So plain hiking descents count as
eccentric training — his Rinjani trek was genuine descent conditioning, though the RBE from
it has almost certainly expired (protection windows run ~3 weeks to 6 months at the outside).

## 3. Practitioner progression (weaker evidence, but usable)

[Run-Ultra RBE protocol](https://run-ultra.com/training/repeated-bout-effect/) — a 16-week
scheme, compressible to 11:

| Block | Segment length | Gradient | Pace | Weekly downhill total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation | 5-10 min | 4-6% | comfortable | 15-20 min |
| Building | 10-20 min | 6-10% | controlled | 25-30 min |
| Intensification | 20-30 min | 8-12% | add faster intervals | 30-40 min |
| Race-specific | 1-2 long race-simulation runs | — | — | taper the volume, keep the stimulus |

Timing: key sessions **3-5 weeks out**; re-expose **every 2-3 weeks**; last hard downhill
session **~2 weeks before race day**.

**Jason Koop dissents on volume** ([CTS](https://trainright.com/downhill-running-training-go-faster-hurt-less/)):
argues **1-2 hard downhill sessions total** is enough to create the adaptation, that everyday
endurance runs on hilly terrain are a sufficient dose, and that you need **2-3 easy days
after a hard downhill effort**. He also explicitly **does not recommend** weighted vests
(2.5-3x force multiplier per pound), tire dragging, or stairs/parking garages as downhill
substitutes ([CTS flat-area article](https://trainright.com/train-for-mountainous-ultramarathon-live-in-flat-area/)).
Note the tension with the 10-session lab finding above — I'd side with more exposure than
Koop suggests given this athlete is completely downhill-naive, but keep each session easy.

Koop's flat-area recommendations that *are* concrete:
- **Mountain training camp, 4-6 weeks out, 2-3 days, all at endurance intensity** — rated the
  single best option.
- Local short hill circuits, **2-3x/week for 4-6 weeks, 15-90 m (50-300 ft) of gain per rep**,
  endurance pace.
- Decline treadmill, 2-3x/week, alternating equal periods of climbing and descending.

## 4. The hard constraint: decline treadmills barely exist

The lab protocols use **-10% to -15%**. Commercial gym treadmills do not go there:
- **Technogym Skillrun** (common in European gyms): **-3%** only, +25% incline.
- Sole TT8: -6%. NordicTrack X24: -6%.
- True -15% to -25% decline requires **h/p/cosmos** or similar lab/rehab treadmills, which
  are rare outside sports-science labs and physio clinics.

**Consequence:** the gold-standard protocol is probably *not* directly executable in
Amsterdam. Worth having him actually check a few gyms (and any university sports-science
lab / rehab clinic) for a decline-capable treadmill, but plan around not finding one.
A -3% treadmill is close to useless for eccentric preparation.

## 5. What's actually available in/near Amsterdam

Ranked by eccentric fidelity to the race demand:

1. **Zuid-Kennemerland National Park dunes** — ~20-25 min by train from Amsterdam
   (Haarlem/Overveen/Zandvoort). **Kopje van Bloemendaal = 50 m**, **Starreberg = 30 m**.
   Dune faces can be genuinely steep (30-45%), which is a *better gradient match* to the
   race's 25-30% descent than anything else available. Marked trails only gain ~90-150 m over
   11-16 km, so **repeats on a single dune face are the mechanism**, not loop running.
   - 40 m dune repeat x 25 = **1,000 m descent** in a session.
   - **Caveat with evidence behind it:** sand is compliant and *reduces* muscle damage
     relative to firm ground ([2017 sand vs grass study](https://coachathletics.com.au/coaching-education/running-on-sand-for-injury-prevention-and-performance)),
     while costing **~1.6x** the metabolic energy. So dunes give great *technique* and
     *aerobic/vertical* work but a **blunted eccentric stimulus**. They are not a complete
     substitute for hard-surface descending, and Madeira is volcanic rock, stone steps and
     roots — hard.
2. **Schoorlse Duinen** — highest dunes in NL, ~1 h from Amsterdam. Better for a long
   vert-focused day.
3. **Utrechtse Heuvelrug** (Amerongen/Rhenen, ~40-60 min by train) and **Posbank /
   Veluwezoom** (~1 h 15, hills to **90 m**) — the best *hard-surface* descents in the
   country, and the closest thing to a real descent. Posbank is the standard Dutch answer to
   this problem.
4. **South Limburg** (Vaalserberg 322 m) — ~2.5 h, worth one weekend trip.
5. **Stairwells / multi-storey car park ramps** — a tall stairwell at ~3 m/floor means a
   20-floor building = 60 m per descent, so **~33 laps for 1,000 m**. Running *down* stairs
   is eccentric and hard-surface, which is on-point; but it's high injury risk, awkward, and
   Koop explicitly rates it poorly. Best used as a supplement, and worth noting Madeira
   courses genuinely contain long stone staircases, so stair descending has real specificity
   here.
6. **Canal bridges / dikes** — 5-10 m of relief. Volume only, negligible eccentric load.

**Recommendation:** the highest-value intervention is almost certainly a
**2-3 day terrain camp 4-6 weeks out** — Ardennes (Belgium, ~3 h), Eifel (Germany, ~4 h) or
a cheap flight to a real mountain. This aligns with Koop's top-rated option and is the only
way to get near race-specific descent volume.

## 6. Gym-based eccentric work — dosage and honest caveats

**Evidence-based dosage:**
- **Downhill-specific eccentric exercises** ([CTS](https://trainright.com/eccentric-strength-training-for-improved-downhill-running-performance/)):
  depth box jumps, lateral skaters, Bulgarian split squats, pistol squats, single-leg skater
  squats.
  - Beginner: **1-2 sets x 5-8 reps at <=70% 1RM, 1-2x/week, 1-3 exercises**.
  - Experienced: **3-5 sets x 8-15 reps at 70-90% 1RM, 2-3x/week**.
  - In-season maintenance: **1-2x/week at 60-70% 1RM**.
- **Tempo:** **3-5 s eccentric (lowering) phase**; progress by adding reps or load every
  **7-10 days**.
- **Slow vs fast eccentrics** ([PMC6410101](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6410101/)):
  slow = **5 x 6 eccentric-only at 90% 1RM, ~4 s per rep** → muscle thickness **+6.0%**;
  fast = **9 x 9 at 70% 1RM, <1 s per rep** → RFD gains but no thickness change. For descent
  durability, **slow/heavy is the right bias**.
- **Flywheel / isoinertial** (if a device is available): **4-7 sets x 7 reps, 3x/week for
  8 weeks** improves running economy and eccentric load capacity in recreational runners.
- **Stair descending as a standalone modality** ([PMC3601017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3601017/)):
  6 weeks of stair *descending* raised eccentric peak torque **+12.3%** (vs +9.6% concentric
  for stair ascending). Low-cost, well-tolerated, eccentric-specific.
- **Recovery:** **48-72 h** between heavy eccentric sessions.

**Exercise selection note with evidence:** the **reverse Nordic curl does NOT produce
superior eccentric quad activation** compared with bodyweight squat-based exercises
([PubMed 39214520](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39214520/)) — don't over-invest in it.
The **decline squat** is the variation that most closely reproduces the downhill quad
loading pattern.

**Where the evidence is genuinely weak:** the transfer from *gym* eccentrics to *downhill
running* damage protection is assumed more than demonstrated. The narrative review notes
that endurance-trained subjects in most studies were **not** familiarised with downhill
running, and that specific downhill exposure "may confer a greater protective mechanism"
than general resistance work. **Gym eccentrics are a supplement to, not a replacement for,
actual descending.** Anyone who tells you squats alone will prepare quads for 2,000 m of
descent is going beyond the data.

## 6b. Recovery from eccentric damage — what has evidence

Relevant because the block will deliberately generate damage, and he is already STRAINED.

| Intervention | Evidence | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Protein supplementation** | [Meta-analysis, EJCN 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41430-022-01250-y) | Preserves MVC at 96 h, **ES 0.563**. Most consistent finding. |
| **Tart cherry juice** | [Meta-analysis, Sports Med Open](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-026-00993-3) | MVC recovery **ES 0.63 immediately post, 1.12 at 24 h**. But high heterogeneity, sensitive to single-study removal, and [null in non-resistance-trained men](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6683053/). Promising, not settled. |
| **High-pressure compression garments** | [Frontiers 2018, trained trail runners, downhill protocol](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.01627/full) | Beneficial for acute and delayed neuromuscular alterations after downhill running; lower soreness at 24 h post-marathon. Cheap, low-risk. |

Adequate protein and sleep do most of the work. The supplements are marginal gains; don't
let them substitute for spacing sessions correctly.

## 7. Damage is driven by speed as much as gradient

- Tissue-stress models predict **>40% higher cumulative damage at 5 m/s vs 3 m/s** on a -10%
  slope. Speed is a *major* lever.
- Steeper is not monotonically worse: at **-10 degrees** the knee's energy absorption is
  *blunted* and load shifts to ankle and hip. A slope shallower than -10% can produce more
  quad damage than a steeper one depending on speed, duration and stride length.
- Metabolic cost is minimised between **-10% and -20%**; steeper than that, braking demand
  pushes cost back up.
- **Practical translation:** on the 25-30% race descent, *velocity control* is the primary
  protective variable. Training should build the ability to descend *controlled*, and the
  race plan should treat the early steep descent as a damage-budget decision.

## 7b. Safety: exertional rhabdomyolysis

[Systematic review, ultra-trail races, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12225290/):

- Reported incidence **0.28%** (1 case / 348 subjects) — but the authors flag this as
  underestimated due to misdiagnosis.
- **CK rose a median 5,370 ± 7,290%** post- vs pre-race (range 295-11,508%).
  **11 of 20 races** produced mean CK meeting standard rhabdomyolysis diagnostic thresholds
  (1,000-10,000 U/L); **4 races hit 14,500-43,762 U/L.**
- Interestingly, **no consistent relationship between descent-to-distance ratio and CK** —
  races with the highest descent ratios (64.6%) did not reliably produce the highest CK.
  So the "more descent = more damage" intuition is not cleanly supported at race level.
- **The listed risk factor that matters here: "lack of specific training for eccentric
  exercise."** Others: dehydration or over-hydration, temperature extremes, medications.
- Authors' recommendation: "**training and accumulating a sufficient eccentric load may be
  vital in preventing runners from suffering ER episodes.**"

This is the strongest safety argument for doing the eccentric block at all: an untrained
athlete taking 2,003 m of descent is precisely the described risk profile. Practical flags
for him: dark urine, disproportionate swelling/stiffness, or severe pain out of line with
the effort — all warrant medical attention rather than a rest day.

## 8. Technique — and a contested point

- **Increasing cadence** reduces per-step peak load and impulse at the patellofemoral joint,
  tibia and Achilles across speeds and gradients. This is the standard advice.
- **But** the 2025 Frontiers review reports that running at a cadence **within ±5% of
  preferred** minimised caloric cost *and* impulse loading, with -10% below preferred being
  clearly worse. It does not support large deliberate cadence increases.
- **Foot strike:** forefoot striking reduces vertical impact force vs rearfoot, especially
  downhill — but deliberately *switching* foot strike showed **no improvement in
  neuromuscular fatigue**. Adaptability across patterns mattered more than any one pattern.
- Real-race data (Giandolini): cadence naturally *drops* on descents as fatigue accumulates
  (180.6 → 174.6 → 163.8 spm across a race).
- **Honest read:** "shorten your stride, quicken your feet, don't brake" is well supported in
  direction but the magnitude is oversold. Don't prescribe a cadence number; prescribe
  *practising* varied descent technique so adaptability develops.

## 9. Recommended structure for 11 weeks (synthesis)

- **Weeks 1-3 (base):** introduce eccentric gym work 2x/week (3-5 s lowering, moderate load).
  One dune/hill session per week, easy, descending controlled. Total downhill 15-20 min/week.
  Expect real soreness from the first 1-2 sessions — that's the point, and it's the most
  valuable single stimulus in the block.
- **Weeks 4-7 (build):** downhill exposure **every week**, aiming toward 10 cumulative
  downhill sessions across the block (the number that actually moved soreness). Progress
  descent volume 25-30 min/week; add hard-surface descents (Heuvelrug/Posbank). Gym
  eccentrics maintained 2x/week, heavy and slow.
- **Weeks 7-8 (peak / camp):** **terrain camp**, 2-3 days, endurance intensity, maximum
  descent volume. This is the single highest-value week.
- **Week 9:** last substantial descent session. Then **3-4 days easy** after it.
- **Weeks 10-11 (taper):** keep one short, easy descent stimulus ~10-14 days out to preserve
  RBE; **no hard eccentric work inside the final 10 days** — DOMS resolves in 4 days but
  strength and RFD deficits are what you're protecting.

  "strong aerobic endurance", 5-10% moderate. In marathoners, the lowest HR-pace decoupling
  predicts holding speed and faster finish times. For a **5-7 hour** mountain race this is the
  metric that should worry us most — more than the descent.
- **Declining VO2max + STRAINED status + a plan that wants to add heavy eccentric load** is a
  bad combination. Eccentric work is uniquely fatiguing and takes 48-72 h to clear.
- **Durability/fatigue-resistance** is built by *volume and long runs at easy intensity*, not
  by more intensity ([UESCA](https://uesca.com/how-to-train-durability-for-trail-ultras/)).
  Runners with higher volume and regular long runs show better running-economy durability.
- **Recommendation:** resolve the strain first. The eccentric block is worth more when it
  lands on a rested athlete, and the RBE is biggest in the naive — it doesn't expire quickly,
  so delaying it 1-2 weeks costs little. Prioritise easy aerobic volume to pull decoupling
  down before layering descent work on top.

**One point in his favour:** at 56 kg / 160 cm his power-to-weight is excellent for climbing
(body mass is a direct multiplier on the mechanical work of lifting the centre of mass), and
low body mass proportionally reduces absolute eccentric braking loads on the descent. The
2,003 m of ascent is the part of this race his physiology is best suited to. Note also that
**plantar-flexor (calf) strength** is an independent determinant of uphill running speed
alongside body weight ([PMC7922024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7922024/))
— worth explicit calf loading in the gym plan.

## 9c. Concrete session templates (Amsterdam-executable)

**A. Preconditioning walk — "the cheap insurance"** *(highest evidence-to-effort ratio)*
- Find the steepest sustained descent available: a steep dune face, a car-park ramp, or a
  stairwell. Target **-25 to -30%**.
- **5 minutes of continuous downhill walking, ~110 m total descent, wearing a pack at 10%
  body mass (≈5.6 kg).** Walk *down* only; walk or ride back up easy.
- Do this **once, early** (week 7-8). Expect 2-4 days of soreness. That soreness is the
  investment.
- Repeat every **2-3 weeks** to hold the protection.

**B. Dune descent repeats — technique + volume**
- Zuid-Kennemerland (Overveen/Bloemendaal), 40 m dune face.
- **15-25 descents** = **600-1,000 m of descent**, with equal ascent, in ~60-90 min.
- Run the descents *controlled*; the aim is repetition and adaptability, not speed.
- **Caveat:** sand blunts the eccentric stimulus. Treat this as skill + aerobic + vertical
  work, and get hard-surface descents elsewhere.

**C. Hard-surface descent day — the specific one**
- Posbank / Veluwezoom (hills to ~110 m) or Utrechtse Heuvelrug (Amerongse Berg).
- Long session with **deliberate repeated descents**, endurance intensity throughout.
- Target **800-1,500 m of descent** in the session. Then **3-4 easy days**.
- Schedule these in weeks 11, 13 and the peak session. This is the closest available
  approximation of race demand.

**D. Stairwell descent — the wet-weather fallback**
- 20-floor stairwell ≈ **60 m per descent** (3 m/floor). **17 laps ≈ 1,000 m.**
- Ride the lift up, walk/jog down, so the session is purely eccentric.
- Add the 10% BW pack to increase force per step.
- Hard surface, steep, weather-independent, and Madeira genuinely has long stone
  staircases — the specificity is better than its reputation.

**E. Gym eccentric block (2x/week, weeks 7-14)**
- Decline squat or step-down: **3-4 x 8-10 per leg, 3-4 s lowering**
- Bulgarian split squat: **3 x 8 per leg, slow eccentric**
- Heavy slow eccentric leg press or squat: **5 x 6 at ~90% 1RM, ~4 s lowering**
- Single-leg calf raise (plantar flexors are an independent uphill determinant):
  **3 x 12 per leg**
- Progress load every 7-10 days. **48-72 h between sessions.** Cut to 1x/week at maintained
  load from ~2 weeks out; **nothing eccentric inside the final 10-14 days.**

## 10. What to tell him honestly

1. Downhill training reduces **soreness and CK** reliably; it does **not** reliably prevent
   **strength loss**. He will arrive at the second climb with meaningfully weaker quads no
   matter what.
2. He is downhill-**naive**, which is actually good news: the RBE is largest in the naive, so
   the first 2-3 sessions buy the most. Getting them in *early* matters more than perfecting
   the rest.
3. The realistic ceiling on flat-terrain preparation is limited. A terrain camp is worth more
   than any amount of gym work.
4. Sand dunes flatter you — they build the engine and the skill but under-load the quads
   relative to Madeira's hard volcanic trail and stone steps.
5. The race-day descent plan is a **co-equal** intervention with training, not an
   afterthought. **And the 11.5-hour cut-off means holding back on the early descent is
   nearly free** — there is ~5 hours of margin against a realistic 5-6.5 h finish. Descending
   the first steep section conservatively costs minutes and protects the remaining 20 km.
   There is no clock-based argument for racing it.
